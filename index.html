Wąsosz in 1906

In 1906, Wąsosz (near Łomża, in northeastern Poland) was under Russian imperial rule, during the upheaval known as the Revolution of 1905–1907. For a Polish family living there, that meant life under a government that restricted Polish national expression, with growing demands for independence or autonomy and rising tension between the authorities and the local population.

The useful question for research into a family from around Wąsosz is what these national events meant for ordinary people in a small town and its surrounding villages.

Who ruled Wąsosz in 1906?

Wąsosz lay in the Congress Kingdom of Poland, created after Napoleon's defeat and increasingly subordinated to the Russian Empire through the nineteenth century. It belonged to the Łomża administrative region.

Sovereign power
Tsar Nicholas II ruled the Russian Empire.
Local administration
Russian imperial officials and institutions held political authority.
National situation
Poland did not exist as an independent state. Russia, Prussia and Austria had partitioned its territories in the late eighteenth century.
Local population
The surrounding countryside was mostly Polish-speaking and Catholic. Jewish communities were an important part of the region's towns and local economy.

Ancestors living here were not in the independent Poland familiar from later family history. They lived in a territory governed by an empire that had suppressed earlier Polish uprisings.

The Revolution of 1905–1907

The most important immediate political event was the revolution that swept the Russian Empire and its Polish territories. These were the main events affecting the region.

  1. 1904–1905
    War with JapanRussia's military defeats weakened confidence in the tsarist government and contributed to widespread unrest.
  2. February 1905
    School protests in ŁomżaStudents demanded changes to the Russian-controlled education system, including greater access and the right to Polish-language education. The movement spread to girls' schools as well as boys' schools.
  3. Late 1905
    A brief opening, then repressionStrikes and demonstrations spread through the Polish territories. The tsar promised civil liberties and a national assembly, but the authorities imposed martial law and arrested activists.
  4. 1906
    Continued unrestPolitical arrests and underground organizing continued. In the first months of the year, Jewish socialist activists associated with the Bund were arrested in Łomża.

What this meant for ordinary families

For a farming or working-class family, political events mattered less as arguments about independence than through their effect on daily life.

Religion and language
Catholic parish life was a central institution for Polish-speaking families. Russian rule made language and national identity political issues, especially in education and administration.
Land and livelihood
Most rural families depended on agriculture, farm labor or small trades. The distribution of land and the relations between peasants and landowners were major social issues.
Military service
The Russian imperial state could conscript eligible men into its army, separating a young man from his family for years.
Political risk
Revolutionary activity could bring surveillance, arrest or punishment. Many ordinary people avoided politics altogether, even when they sympathized with Polish national aspirations.

These are regional and imperial circumstances. They are not evidence that any particular family in Wąsosz took part in political activity.

The Jewish community

Wąsosz and nearby Łomża had a long-standing Jewish presence. In Łomża, Jewish residents took part in political life through movements that included the General Jewish Labour Bund, which organized Jewish workers and advocated socialist and cultural rights.

The Bund's activity, and the arrests of its members in Łomża in 1905–1906, show that the upheaval involved more than Polish nationalists and Russian authorities. Jewish socialists and other groups with different political goals were part of it too.

What happened next

The revolution was gradually suppressed by 1907. Russian rule continued until the First World War transformed the region. Poland regained independence in 1918, and Wąsosz became part of the restored Polish state.
